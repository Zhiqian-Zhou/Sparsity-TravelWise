\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A typical European railway operator runs several thousand passenger services per day. About one in ten of those services is \emph{disrupted} -- it arrives more than five minutes late or is cancelled outright -- and most of those disruptions are predictable from information that is already available before the train leaves the platform. If an operator could rank the next-day timetable by disruption probability, ground crews could pre-stage at the high-risk stations, the call centre could pre-warn the affected passengers, and the timetable team could reroute around the rolling stock that is most likely to fail. The same probability, computed again at each stop along the route, becomes a continuous \emph{inflight} risk score that the dispatcher can act on minute by minute.

This is, in principle, a textbook spatio-temporal classification problem. In practice three things make it harder than it looks.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The three operators speak three different data languages.} Italy publishes a flat per-stop table where missing values are encoded as the literal strings \texttt{N} (origin stop) and \texttt{S} (cancellation). Finland publishes one row per service with every stop nested inside a serialised Python literal, weather observations and all. The Netherlands publishes a wide-tariff CSV with three separate cancellation flags and no embedded weather at all. Even the meaning of ``disrupted'' shifts: in Italy it is implicit in a delay column, in Finland it is a structured flag, in the Netherlands it is the union of three different indicators. Before any model can be trained, those three formats must be harmonised into a single Common Data Model.
    \item \textbf{Disruptions are rare and unevenly rare.} Across the held-out test month (June 2024) only about \SI{8}{\percent} of stops are disrupted, but the rate rises sharply on bad-weather days in Finland. A model that simply guesses the majority class would be \SI{92}{\percent} accurate, which is why we report PR-AUC and per-class~F1 throughout this paper instead of accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Cause labels exist for one country only.} The Netherlands publishes a separate disruption-incident log that tags each event with a cause group such as \emph{rolling stock} or \emph{weather}. Italy and Finland do not. Any cause-prediction model is therefore trained on Dutch labels and evaluated on Italian and Finnish stops without an in-target ground truth -- the textbook setup for a domain-shift failure, and (\cref{sec:transfer}) we document exactly such a failure.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes five concrete contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A unified, anti-leakage feature store.} We harmonise \num{16586834} stop rows across Italy, Finland and the Netherlands into a single Common Data Model with 37 features for the pre-departure scenario and 40 for the inflight scenario (\cref{sec:data,sec:features}). Six independent layers prevent test-set information from leaking into training features, including a unit-tested invariant on every lag feature.
    \item \textbf{A four-model benchmark, twice.} Logistic Regression, LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}, XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and GraphSAGE~\cite{hamilton2017inductive} are each trained twice on identical splits -- once with pre-departure features only (scenario A) and once with three additional inflight features (scenario B). The inflight uplift, dominated by a single feature, is dramatic and almost entirely attributable to within-run delay propagation (\cref{sec:models,sec:evaluation}).
    \item \textbf{TreeSHAP attributions on the full test set.} We run TreeSHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020local} on every one of the \num{2303236} test stops -- not a sample -- and complement the per-row attributions with a lag-feature ablation. The ablation isolates the share of the inflight uplift that is caused by lag features versus inflight features (\cref{sec:xai}).
    \item \textbf{An embedded Knowledge Graph that links predictions to context.} The model's per-row attributions feed a queryable graph database with \num{1217406} services, \num{2399} stations and \num{16586834} stop relationships. A bridge query takes a \emph{(service, station, date)} triple from the SHAP report and returns the surrounding spatial-temporal neighbourhood: this stop's actual delay, recent faults at this station, and adjacent stations. The graph runs on Sparksee~\cite{sparksee2024,martinez2017dex} via its Maven-Central JVM distribution and answers the bridge query in \SI{7.7}{ms} (\cref{sec:kg}).
    \item \textbf{A cause-of-disruption classifier with documented transfer limits.} A multi-class classifier with cause-specific feature engineering and symmetric sample weighting reaches a test macro-F1 of \num{0.213} on Dutch cause labels; a single engineered interaction (\texttt{winter\_x\_severe\_weather}) takes the rare \emph{weather} class to F1$=$\num{0.509}. We then transfer to Italy and Finland and document, with root-cause analysis, exactly why the transfer to Finland never predicts weather despite Finland's harsher winter (\cref{sec:cause,sec:transfer}).
\end{enumerate}

The full pipeline -- ingestion, features, four-model benchmark, SHAP attributions, the Knowledge Graph and the cause classifier -- is reproducible from raw open data with three shell commands. Implementation rests on pandas, scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} and PyTorch Geometric~\cite{fey2019fast}; a 30-test pytest suite enforces the anti-leakage and split invariants that the rest of the paper relies on.
